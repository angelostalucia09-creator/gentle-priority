Separation Anxiety: It's Not Just for Children

Based on research from the American Psychiatric Association & NIMH

What Is Separation Anxiety Disorder?

Separation Anxiety Disorder involves excessive fear or anxiety about being separated from those to whom an individual is emotionally attached — far beyond what is considered age-appropriate. While commonly diagnosed in children, the American Psychiatric Association confirms that adults can experience it too. It is more than just missing someone — it is intense distress that affects daily functioning, school, work, and relationships.

Signs & Symptoms

  • Persistent, excessive worry about harm coming to loved ones when you are apart
  • Fear that something terrible will happen that separates you from them
  • Refusal or reluctance to be alone or away from home without that person
  • Physical complaints — headaches, stomach aches, nausea — when separation happens or is expected
  • Repeated nightmares or bad dreams about being separated
  • Extreme distress, panic, or sadness during actual or anticipated separation

Common Causes & Triggers

It can develop or be triggered by:

  • Major life changes — moving, changing schools or jobs, starting a new relationship
  • Loss or grief — the death of a loved one, a breakup, or a long separation
  • Previous experiences of being separated unexpectedly or feeling abandoned
  • Family history — having relatives with anxiety disorders may increase risk
  • Stressful or traumatic events that happened while away from loved ones

What Helps

  1. Practice gentle separations: Start with very short periods apart and gradually increase them. Consistency helps build trust that you will be reunited.
  2. Create a reassuring routine: A calm goodbye ritual helps signal that you are leaving but will return. Keep it brief and positive.
  3. Stay connected in healthy ways: Knowing you can check in briefly provides comfort without becoming a barrier to building independence.
  4. Challenge anxious thoughts: Ask yourself: "What am I afraid will happen? Is that likely? What's the evidence?"
  5. Seek professional support: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is effective. It helps you build confidence in your ability to be safe on your own and develop healthy coping strategies.
💚 Remember: Wanting to be close to the people you love is natural and beautiful — it shows how deeply you care. It becomes a concern only when the fear of being apart stops you from living your life fully. You don't have to "tough it out" alone. With patience, understanding, and the right support, you can learn to feel safe and secure — both when you're together and when you're apart.

Sources: American Psychiatric Association (DSM-5), National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry, Mayo Clinic
